Kidney health guide

Everything a patient should know about kidney disease, in plain language. Start with the stages below, then read each topic.

The five stages of chronic kidney disease

Stage 1eGFR 90 or aboveNormal filtering, with signs of kidney damage
Stage 2eGFR 60 to 89Mild reduction in kidney function
Stage 3eGFR 30 to 59Moderate reduction. Treatment matters most here
Stage 4eGFR 15 to 29Severe reduction. Time to plan ahead
Stage 5eGFR below 15Kidney failure. Dialysis or transplant is discussed

Know your numbers

eGFR

Shows how well your kidneys filter. A value below 60 for three months or more points to chronic kidney disease.

Urine albumin ratio

Detects protein leaking into the urine, often the earliest sign. Diabetics should check it every year.

Blood pressure

Many people with kidney disease are advised a target near 130/80. Your doctor will set yours.

How your kidneys work

You have two kidneys, each about the size of a fist, one on either side of the spine just below the ribs. They work around the clock.

What the kidneys do

  • Filter waste and extra water out of the blood and pass them out as urine
  • Help control blood pressure
  • Keep salts such as sodium and potassium in balance
  • Make a hormone that tells the body to produce red blood cells
  • Help keep bones strong by activating vitamin D

Why kidney disease is easy to miss

  • The kidneys can lose a large part of their function before you feel anything
  • This is why a simple blood and urine test matters if you are at risk

Living with one kidney

  • Many people live a normal, healthy life with one working kidney, for example after donating one

Warning signs and tests

Early kidney disease often causes no symptoms at all, so a simple blood and urine test is the only way to know.

Signs that need attention

  • Swelling of the feet, ankles or around the eyes
  • Foamy urine, or blood in the urine
  • Passing much less urine, or getting up often at night to pass urine
  • Tiredness, poor appetite, nausea or itching all over
  • Blood pressure that is hard to control
  • Breathlessness when lying flat

Who should be tested every year

  • People with diabetes or high blood pressure
  • Anyone with a family history of kidney disease
  • People over 60
  • People who take painkillers such as diclofenac or ibuprofen for long periods
  • People with kidney stones or frequent urine infections

Tests you may be asked for

  • Serum creatinine and eGFR, which show how well the kidneys filter
  • Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, which picks up protein leak early
  • Urine routine and microscopy
  • Ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder
  • Blood pressure and blood sugar

Go to the emergency department if

  • You pass very little or no urine
  • You are very breathless, confused or have chest pain
  • You have severe back pain with fever and vomiting

Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

CKD means the kidneys have been damaged or working below normal for three months or more. It usually progresses slowly, and the right care can slow it down a great deal.

Common causes

  • Diabetes
  • High blood pressure
  • Inflammation of the kidney filters (glomerulonephritis)
  • Polycystic kidney disease and other inherited conditions
  • Long-standing blockage from stones or prostate problems
  • Long-term use of painkillers and some other medicines

How CKD is treated

  • Keeping blood pressure and blood sugar within your target
  • Medicines that protect the kidneys, chosen by your doctor for your case
  • Treating anaemia, bone and mineral problems and acid build-up
  • Avoiding painkillers that harm the kidneys
  • Regular blood and urine tests to track progress

Planning ahead

  • At stage 4, you and your doctor discuss dialysis and transplant options early, so nothing is rushed
  • Not everyone with CKD reaches kidney failure; many stay stable for years

Diabetes, blood pressure and the kidneys

Diabetes and high blood pressure are the two most common causes of kidney disease. Both damage the tiny filters in the kidney, and kidney damage in turn raises blood pressure.

The earliest sign

  • A small amount of protein (albumin) in the urine, long before symptoms or a change in creatinine
  • Everyone with diabetes should have a urine albumin test and an eGFR at least once a year

What protects your kidneys

  • Steady blood sugar, checked with HbA1c as often as your doctor advises
  • Blood pressure at the target your doctor sets for you
  • Taking your medicines every day, even when you feel well
  • Reducing salt, not smoking and staying active

Please remember

  • Do not stop insulin or diabetes tablets on your own
  • Tell every doctor you see that you have kidney disease, so doses can be adjusted

Diet and lifestyle

Small daily habits make a real difference. Your diet should be set by your blood reports, so ask before making big changes.

For everyone

  • Keep salt below about one teaspoon (5 g) a day, including pickles, papad and packaged food
  • Stay active for around 30 minutes on most days
  • Keep a healthy weight and do not smoke
  • Avoid over-the-counter painkillers and unverified herbal or ayurvedic medicines

If you already have kidney disease

  • Potassium: if your blood potassium is high, you may be asked to limit tender coconut water, banana, potato and similar foods
  • Phosphorus: may need limiting later, together with medicines taken with meals
  • Protein: moderate amounts, not too much and not too little
  • Fluids: restrict only if your doctor tells you to

Every year

  • Blood pressure, blood sugar, creatinine and eGFR, and urine protein

Dialysis

Dialysis does part of the kidneys’ work when they can no longer keep you well. It is a treatment, not a cure, and many people live well on dialysis for years.

Haemodialysis

  • A machine cleans the blood, usually three times a week for about four hours
  • Needs an access, most often a fistula made in the forearm
  • Do not allow blood pressure checks or blood draws on the fistula arm, and feel for the buzz (thrill) every day

Peritoneal dialysis

  • Done at home, using the lining of the abdomen as the filter
  • A soft tube (catheter) is placed in the abdomen; fluid exchanges are done several times a day or overnight by a machine
  • Suits people who want more freedom and are comfortable learning the steps

Living on dialysis

  • A kidney diet and a daily fluid limit, set for you by your doctor
  • Regular blood tests, and medicines for anaemia and bones
  • Some people choose conservative care without dialysis; this is also discussed openly

Kidney transplant

For many people with kidney failure, a transplant gives the best health and quality of life. A kidney can come from a living donor or a deceased donor.

Before the transplant

  • Blood group and tissue matching
  • Heart, lung and infection checks for the patient, and full health checks for the donor
  • Legal approvals as required for organ donation
  • A transplant before starting dialysis is possible in some cases

After the transplant

  • Anti-rejection medicines must be taken every day for life
  • Extra care to avoid infections in the first months
  • Regular blood tests and clinic visits, with more frequent visits at the start
  • Most people return to normal daily activities

Kidney stones

Stones form when minerals in the urine clump together. They are common, painful and often come back, but they can be prevented.

Symptoms

  • Sudden, severe pain in the side or back that spreads to the groin
  • Blood in the urine, nausea or vomiting
  • A burning feeling or frequent need to pass urine

Treatment

  • Small stones often pass with plenty of fluids and pain relief
  • Larger stones may need shock-wave therapy (lithotripsy) or removal with a thin scope (ureteroscopy)
  • Fever with pain, or being unable to pass urine, needs urgent care

Prevention

  • Drink enough water to pass about 2.5 litres of urine a day, unless you have been told to limit fluids
  • Cut down on salt and excess meat
  • Do not avoid calcium in food; it actually helps
  • Ask for a stone analysis so advice can match your stone type

Acute kidney injury

Acute kidney injury (AKI) means the kidneys suddenly lose function over hours or days. With prompt treatment it is often reversible.

Common triggers

  • Dehydration from vomiting, diarrhoea or heat
  • Severe infection or low blood pressure
  • Painkillers, some antibiotics and herbal products
  • Contrast dye used in scans
  • Blockage of the urine flow

Treatment

  • Finding and treating the cause
  • Careful fluid management and stopping harmful medicines
  • Temporary dialysis in some cases while the kidneys recover

How to lower the risk

  • Drink fluids when you have diarrhoea or vomiting, and see a doctor early
  • Avoid painkillers like diclofenac and ibuprofen unless your doctor agrees
  • Follow up with kidney tests after any episode of AKI

Approach to care

Kidney disease is a long-term condition, so the relationship between doctor and patient matters as much as the prescription. Dr. Sunil Kumar explains each report in simple language, sets realistic targets with the patient and family, and plans ahead so that no decision about dialysis or transplant is rushed.

Clinical interests

  • Early detection and slowing of chronic kidney disease
  • Diabetic and hypertensive kidney disease
  • Glomerular diseases and protein in the urine
  • Dialysis planning and access care
  • Kidney transplant evaluation and follow-up

What to expect at your first visit

  1. Bring all previous reports, scans and your medicines
  2. Your history, blood pressure, weight and swelling are checked
  3. Blood and urine tests or an ultrasound are advised if needed
  4. You leave with a clear plan: medicines, diet, targets and your next visit

Common questions

Short answers to what patients ask most.

When should I see a nephrologist?

See one if you have swelling, foamy or bloody urine, a high creatinine or low eGFR, protein in the urine, or diabetes or high blood pressure that has lasted for years.

Will I need dialysis if my creatinine is high?

Not necessarily. It depends on your eGFR, symptoms and the cause. Many people with kidney disease never need dialysis.

Can kidney disease be reversed?

Sudden injury can often recover. Long-term chronic disease usually cannot be reversed, but the right care can slow it down a great deal.

Are painkillers safe for my kidneys?

Regular use of painkillers such as diclofenac or ibuprofen can harm the kidneys. Ask your doctor before taking them for more than a day or two.

Can I drink tender coconut water?

It is rich in potassium. If your blood potassium is normal it is usually fine in moderation, but if it is high your doctor may ask you to limit it. Check with your doctor.

What should I bring to the first visit?

Bring all previous reports and scans, and the medicine strips or prescriptions you take now.
